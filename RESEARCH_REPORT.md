# Does Temporal Context Help Frame Retrieval for Embodied Memory? A Budget-Matched Study on FindingDory

*Retrieval-only study. Date: 2026-11-20. Status: exploratory research prototype.
All numbers are generated by the scripts in this repository (`results/tables/`).*

## 1. Abstract
Embodied agents that act on long histories must decide which past observations to keep in a limited
context. Using the FindingDory benchmark's released ground-truth goal frames, we compare frame
selectors under a fixed budget of k frames out of each 96-frame experience video: uniform sampling
(the benchmark paper's strategy), random sampling, CLIP semantic top-k, and a proposed extension,
Temporal-Neighborhood Retrieval (TNR). TNR spends half the budget on semantic anchors and half on
their temporal neighbours. On all 100 validation episodes (5,595 questions), TNR **reduces** goal
coverage by 11.7 percentage points relative to semantic top-k at k = 8 (95% episode-bootstrap CI
[−12.7, −10.7]), at every budget and in every task category, with the largest loss on temporal tasks.
Precision is unchanged. The loss comes from redundancy: ground-truth labels are contiguous runs, so
neighbours rarely reach a goal the anchor did not. Two further findings matter more than the method
itself. First, CLIP semantic top-k is no better than uniform sampling overall (+0.3 pp). Second, a
fixed question-independent prompt describing manipulation beats the actual question by 13.1 pp, so
most of CLIP's value here is a generic prior, not question understanding. A post-hoc analysis shows
the inverse of our hypothesis helps: enforcing temporal *diversity* improves semantic retrieval by
7.9 pp (11.2 pp on temporal tasks). We report the original hypothesis as a negative result.

## 2. Introduction and motivation
A memory-equipped agent cannot feed every past frame to a reasoning model. FindingDory (Yadav et al.,
2026) shows that frozen VLMs do not improve, and often get worse, as more uniformly subsampled frames
are added (its Fig. 4a), and calls for memory mechanisms that extract task-relevant information. A
common answer is retrieval: embed the frames and keep those most similar to the instruction. Image-text
similarity is computed frame by frame, so we hypothesised that it misses temporal context. For example,
"the receptacle you placed an object on right before interacting with the can" depends on what
happened around the matching frames. Spending part of the budget on frames adjacent to the best
semantic matches might keep that context.

## 3. Related work: FindingDory
*Findings reported by the original paper (not reproduced here):*
- FindingDory has two phases in Habitat/HSSD. An oracle robot first performs 2–11 pick-and-place
  interactions (400–3,500 frames). The agent then receives the history and must navigate according to
  one of 60 templated instructions in 11 categories (spatial, temporal, multi-goal). There are 1,478
  train and 100 val episodes, and 79k/5.9k instructions.
- Baselines are hierarchical: a VLM selects goal frames from a uniformly subsampled video, then an
  ImageNav or mapping policy navigates. Frozen VLMs reach 13–27% high-level success, and SFT
  Qwen2.5-VL-3B on 96-frame videos reaches about 50%. Multi-goal tasks are near zero for frozen VLMs.
- Ground-truth frames are poses that satisfy the task's goal conditions (distance, angle, semantic-mask
  coverage, room). For the 96-frame release they are remapped to subsampled indices.

*What the paper does not evaluate, as far as we could find:* query-conditioned frame retrieval
(e.g. CLIP top-k), temporal-neighbourhood expansion, or retrieval quality separated from VLM
reasoning. It cites retrieval-style memory systems (ReMEmbR, Embodied-RAG, MobilityVLA) without
benchmarking them. Neither CLIP-based frame retrieval nor temporal context expansion is new in
general (both are standard in video RAG and moment retrieval). Our contribution is a controlled,
budget-matched test of these choices on an embodied-memory benchmark with per-category analysis.
See `docs/paper_notes.md`.

## 4. Research question and hypothesis
**Primary (pre-specified):** at k = 8, does TNR (r = 2, anchor fraction 0.5) achieve higher goal
coverage than semantic top-k, overall and per high-level category?

**Secondary:** (1) larger gains on temporal tasks? (2) dependence on budget k ∈ {4, 8, 16, 32}?
(3) best radius r ∈ {0, 1, 2, 4}? (4) does expansion add irrelevant frames (precision)?
(5) dependence on episode complexity (number of interactions)?

The hypothesis predicted TNR > semantic, especially on temporal tasks. All settings were fixed in
`configs/experiment.yaml` before the main run.

## 5. Methodology
- **Memory.** Each episode's 96 frames (640×480, 1 fps) are encoded once with OpenCLIP ViT-B/32
  (`laion2b_s34b_b79k`). The burned-in "Frame / Time of Day" overlay is blacked out so similarity
  cannot come from rendered text. Portrait frames are padded to square rather than center-cropped.
  Questions are embedded without the constant prefix "The robot's goal is:". Similarity is the cosine
  between unit-normalised embeddings. Embeddings are cached per episode and encoder configuration.
- **No label leakage.** Retrievers see only frame embeddings, the query embedding and k. Ground truth
  is used only by the metric functions afterwards.
- **Output contract** (enforced in `Retriever.retrieve` and tested): exactly min(k, #valid) unique,
  valid frame indices in chronological order, with ranking, scores, latency and config metadata.

## 6. Dataset and evaluation design
- **Data.** `yali30/findingdory-subsampled-96` at revision `95afe1e8`, official validation split: 100
  episodes and 5,876 questions. 5,595 questions have frame targets: 2,666 spatial, 2,029 temporal and
  900 multi-goal. The remaining 281 have no frame target (226 `[[-1]]` for never-observed targets,
  55 `[[]]`) and are analysed separately (§9.7). No model was trained, so the train split is unused.
  A 30-episode dev subset, stratified by number of interactions with seed 0, was inspected during
  development. The other **70 episodes serve as a held-out check**.
- **Metrics** (`src/evaluation/metrics.py`). For a selected set S and per-goal valid lists G_g:
  - *Goal coverage* (primary): the fraction of goals with S ∩ G_g ≠ ∅. Goal identity is kept, so it
    equals any-hit for single-goal questions. It mirrors the paper's SFT protocol, where a chosen frame
    is correct if it lies in the goal's valid list.
  - *All-goals*: every goal covered (strict multi-goal success).
  - *Precision*: |S ∩ ∪G| / k. It is biased upward for any selector that picks adjacent frames,
    because labels are contiguous runs.
  - *Latency*: selection time only, with embeddings precomputed.
- **Label geometry matters.** Valid sets are short contiguous runs (6–31 frames per question,
  1.5–9 runs). At k = 8, random selection hits at least one valid frame 38–93% of the time depending on
  category, so we always report random and uniform floors.
- **Statistics.** Questions in an episode share a video, so all CIs come from an **episode cluster
  bootstrap** (5,000 resamples of episodes) on the pooled question mean. Method comparisons are
  **paired** per question. p-values are two-sided bootstrap p-values. Only the primary comparison is
  confirmatory; everything else is exploratory, with no multiplicity correction.

## 7. Baseline implementations
| Name | Definition |
|---|---|
| Uniform | centres of k equal bins over the valid frames: ⌊(j + 0.5)·T/k⌋ |
| Random | k distinct frames, numpy `default_rng` seeded by (seed, episode, query). Seeds 0–9; we report the per-question mean and the across-seed SD (≤ 0.53 pp) |
| Semantic top-k | top-k cosine similarity, ties broken by earlier index |
| Generic-prompt controls | semantic top-k and TNR using the fixed text "a robot gripper picking up or placing an object" for every question |

The generic prompt is a diagnostic for "does the question matter?". It was written after Phase 2/3
inspection of the dev subset, which showed that labels sit on manipulation segments. It uses no labels
at retrieval time, but it is informed by knowledge of the label structure, so it is not a fair
competing method.

## 8. Temporal-Neighborhood Retrieval
`src/memory/temporal_retriever.py`. Given budget k, radius r and anchor fraction f:
1. Anchors A are the top ⌈f·k⌉ frames by similarity. These are the first picks of semantic top-k.
2. Candidates N are frames within ±r of any anchor that are valid and not anchors. Overlaps are
   deduplicated by construction.
3. N is ranked by (distance to nearest anchor ↑, similarity ↓, index ↑): proximity first, relevance as
   a tie-break.
4. The remaining k − |A| slots are filled from N. If N runs out (edges, overlapping anchors, small r),
   the rest are backfilled with the next-best semantic frames.
5. The k unique frames are returned in chronological order.

TNR and semantic top-k share the same anchors and the same budget. They differ only in how slots
⌈f·k⌉+1 … k are spent, which isolates the effect of temporal context. With r = 0, N is empty and TNR
equals semantic top-k exactly (unit-tested and confirmed empirically: difference 0.0 at every k).
Because proximity is ranked first, at f = 0.5 the distance-1 neighbours usually fill every free slot,
so r ≥ 2 changes the output only when anchors cluster or f is small. This is visible in the ablation.
We did not implement the optional soft score α·sim + (1 − α)·proximity: the simpler method already
answered the question, and a soft score would mainly interpolate between the two hard variants.

Tests (`tests/test_retrievers.py`, 134 tests in total) cover budget conservation, duplicates,
sequence boundaries, small episodes, overlapping neighbourhoods, r = 0 equivalence, deterministic
ordering, and missing or corrupted frames.

## 9. Experimental results
Hardware: Apple M3 Pro (MPS). Encoding all 9,600 frames took 82 s once. The full grid (604,260
retrievals) runs in about 21 s.

### 9.2 Experiment 2: memory budget — Fig. 2
TNR − semantic goal coverage (Overall): k = 4: −11.0 [−12.0, −10.0]; k = 8: −11.7; k = 16: −11.7;
k = 32: −7.7 [−8.6, −6.7]. The hypothesis that temporal context helps most under tight budgets is not
supported. At k = 4, TNR uses 2 anchors plus 2 neighbours and loses as much as at k = 8. Semantic
top-k's advantage over uniform is largest at small k (k = 4: 46.1% vs 35.9%) and reverses by k = 32
(90.4% vs 95.3%), where evenly spread frames cover most label runs.

### 9.3 Experiment 3: radius and anchor-fraction ablation — Fig. 3
Overall TNR − semantic at k = 8:

| r \ f | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 (≡ semantic) | 0 | 0 |
| 1 | −9.5 | −11.6 | −4.9 |
| 2 | −20.3 | −11.7 | −4.9 |
| 4 | −21.6 | −11.7 | −4.9 |

There is a clean dose-response: the more budget goes to neighbours, the larger the loss (f = 0.25 with
r = 4 gives 6 neighbour slots and loses 21.6 pp). No radius > 0 helps. At f ≥ 0.5, r ≥ 2 rarely
changes the selection because distance-1 neighbours fill the free slots (§8). The optimal radius is
0, i.e. no temporal expansion.

- **Secondary Q1 (temporal tasks benefit more?): no.** TNR's loss is largest on temporal categories.
- Semantic retrieval clearly beats uniform only where the instruction names a visible entity (Object
  Recall +14.5, Object Attributes +16.5, Conditional +8.2). There, and only there, the question beats
  the generic prompt or ties with it. For ordinal and temporal wording ("second", "before", "at 7:42")
  CLIP's question embedding is worse than a generic manipulation prompt.

### 9.5 Secondary Q4: does expansion add irrelevant frames?
No. TNR − semantic precision at k = 8 is −0.1 pp [−0.5, +0.3]: neighbours are as often valid as
semantic ranks 5–8. The loss comes from **redundancy**, not irrelevance (Fig. 5):
- TNR's 8 frames span 3.4 distinct temporal segments on average, against 6.7 for semantic, 7.4 for
  random and 8.0 for uniform.
- With the same 4 anchors, semantic's next 4 frames add +15.1 pp coverage and TNR's 4 neighbours add
  +3.4 pp.
- When all 4 anchors miss (2,579 questions), semantic's next 4 frames rescue 30.7% and TNR's
  neighbours 7.3%. Under contiguous labels, a neighbour of a hit is in the same goal run, and a
  neighbour of a miss is usually a miss.

### 9.6 Secondary Q5: episode complexity
TNR − semantic does not vary with the number of interactions (episode-level Spearman ρ = 0.15,
p = 0.15; per-level differences −8.7 to −13.7 pp). Semantic − uniform increases with complexity
(ρ = 0.29, p = 0.004). With 2 interactions uniform reaches 86.6%, because labels cover a large share of
a short interaction history. With more interactions semantic catches up and overtakes it.

### 9.7 Special answers
The 281 questions with `[[-1]]` or `[[]]` cannot be scored as retrieval. We asked whether the maximum
question-frame similarity could flag them as unanswerable within the same template. It cannot: AUROC
0.48 (task_10), 0.51 (task_11), 0.48 (task_22) and 0.39 (task_4). Task_30, "a room you did not visit",
is always `[[-1]]`. Detecting "not in memory" would need reasoning, not similarity.

### 9.8 Latency (selection only)
Median per query at k = 8: uniform 0.004 ms, semantic 0.008 ms, random 0.011 ms, TNR 0.018 ms. These
are negligible next to the one-off encoding (82 s for 100 episodes on MPS, about 8.5 ms per frame).

### 9.9 Robustness to the encoder
We repeated the core comparisons with a roughly 4× larger encoder, OpenCLIP **ViT-L/14**
(`laion2b_s32b_b82k`), using the same preprocessing, prompt and questions, at k ∈ {4, 8, 16}
(`results/tables/robustness_ViT-L-14_laion2b_s32b_b82k.csv`). Every conclusion replicates:

| at k = 8 (pp) | ViT-B/32 | ViT-L/14 |
|---|---|---|
| semantic coverage | 61.2 | 62.5 |
| TNR − semantic | −11.7 [−12.7, −10.7] | −12.1 [−13.1, −11.1] |
| semantic − uniform | +0.3 [−3.0, +3.6] | +1.5 [−1.6, +4.7] |
| semantic − generic prompt | −13.1 [−15.5, −10.6] | −11.7 [−14.0, −9.4] |

With ViT-L/14, TNR − semantic is −11.7 / −12.1 / −12.2 pp at k = 4 / 8 / 16, again largest on temporal
(−14.3) and multi-goal (−14.5) tasks. The bigger encoder improves semantic retrieval by only 1.3 pp, and
the question still loses to the generic prompt. So the bottleneck is how instructions relate to frames,
not encoder capacity.

## 10. Ablation and post-hoc analysis
**Post-hoc (not pre-specified): temporal diversity.** Since redundancy explains TNR's loss, we tested
the opposite policy: semantic top-k with temporal non-maximum suppression (skip a frame within ±g of
an already chosen frame, then backfill). Gains at k = 8 over semantic top-k (Fig. 6,
`results/tables/posthoc_diversity.csv`):

| gap g | Overall | Spatial | Temporal | Multi-goal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | +3.6 [+3.1, +4.3] | +2.8 | +4.7 | +3.8 |
| 2 | +5.8 [+4.9, +6.8] | +4.1 | +7.8 | +6.5 |
| 4 | +7.9 [+6.6, +9.2] | +5.5 | **+11.2** [+9.1, +13.4] | +7.7 |
| 8 | +6.3 [+4.7, +8.0] | +3.9 | +9.5 | +6.3 |

Every gap helps, and the same holds with the generic prompt (+4.9 to +8.1 pp). Semantic + NMS (g = 4)
beats uniform at k = 4, 8 and 16 (50.3 / 69.1 / 85.0% vs 35.9 / 60.9 / 78.2%), but not at k = 32
(92.5 vs 95.3%). Caveat: g = 4 is the best of four values chosen after seeing results. It is
optimistic and needs confirmation on fresh data, e.g. the train split, which this project did not use.

## 11. Failure analysis — Experiment 5
Outcome cells for single-goal questions at k = 8 (any-hit; `e5_outcome_cells_k8.csv`): both succeed
50.0%, both fail 34.6%, **semantic succeeds and TNR fails 13.5%**, TNR succeeds and semantic fails 1.9%.
We drew two examples per cell at random (seed 0, distinct episodes; `results/figures/qualitative/`):
- *TNR wins* (ep_87 task_13, "a cabinet you placed an object on"): a semantic anchor at frame 30 sits
  one frame before the valid run 31–34, and its neighbour 31 hits. This is the boundary near-miss TNR
  was designed for, and it is rare.
- *TNR loses* (ep_56 task_43 "the receptacle you placed an object on 4 interactions before … electric
  kettle", valid 26–29; ep_14 task_31 "the first object you interacted with", valid 22–27): semantic's
  lower-ranked picks (28 and 26) land in the short valid run. TNR spends those slots next to wrong
  anchors (16–17, 76–78, 82–83 / 39–43, 68–70).
- *Both fail* (ep_76 task_40 "the object you interacted with between the interactions with knife and
  pen", valid 22–24; ep_15 task_13 "a table you placed an object on", valid 17–28): similarity peaks on
  other manipulation segments. The answer depends on event order, which neither method represents.
- *Both succeed* (ep_6 task_33, ep_29 task_12): broad or visually distinctive targets.
- From Phase 3 (ep_2 task_1, "Navigate to a can"): a frame clearly showing the can is *not* valid,
  because the can was later moved and labels encode its final location. Object-recall labels are
  state-dependent in a way that frame similarity cannot resolve.

## 12. Limitations
- **Retrieval only.** We measure whether valid goal viewpoints are kept in memory, not whether a VLM
  answers correctly (Phase 8 not run, §13) and not embodied success (no Habitat). Good retrieval is
  necessary but not sufficient for the paper's HL-SR.
- **Labels are pose-feasibility sets on 96 subsampled frames.** Results may differ on full-length
  videos, where adjacent frames are near duplicates and the redundancy effect may be stronger.
- **One encoder family** (CLIP ViT-B/32, plus the ViT-L/14 check in §9.9). Video-language or captioning
  encoders may behave differently.
- **The generic prompt was informed by inspecting the label structure** on the dev subset. It is a
  diagnostic, not a deployable method.
- **Exploratory secondary analyses** without multiplicity correction. The post-hoc NMS gap was chosen
  after seeing results.
- **Coverage treats goals as equally weighted** and ignores ordering for Ordered Revisitation.
- **The official relaxed-score function has a quirk:** an empty prediction for a goal is silently
  ignored (`docs/paper_notes.md`). We did not reuse it.

## 13. Future research directions
1. **Phase 8, downstream VLM test (not run).** Feed each selector's k frames, with their indices, to
   a multi-image VLM (e.g. Qwen2.5-VL-3B/7B, as in the paper's baselines) using an identical prompt,
   and score with the paper's single-frame-per-goal protocol. We skipped it because retrieval-only
   results already reject the hypothesis and because running a 3–7B VLM over 5.6k questions per method
   is many GPU-hours. A 30-episode run on a single GPU (or the 48 GB Mac mini) is the natural next step.
2. **Confirm temporal diversity on fresh data:** train split episodes, other gaps, and comparison with
   determinantal or MMR-style diversity.
3. **Event-aware memory:** segment the video into interaction events (e.g. gripper-state changes) and
   retrieve events rather than frames. The labels' contiguous-run structure suggests events are the
   natural memory unit.
4. **Order-aware retrieval** for temporal templates: parse the ordinal or relational constraint and
   retrieve over an event index rather than embedding the whole instruction.
5. **Abstention:** detect when the target was never observed (`[[-1]]`), which similarity cannot do.
